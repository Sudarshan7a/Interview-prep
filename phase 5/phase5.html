First 30 Days — Prove Yourself Fast

The first month sets your reputation for the next 2 years. Engineers who are visible, proactive, and deliver early get the interesting projects. The ones who stay quiet and wait for direction stay junior longer.

⚠️ The biggest mistake new engineers make in week 1
Trying to impress everyone by showing off everything you know. The engineers who advance fastest are the ones who listen 80% of the time in the first month, ask smart questions, and deliver the small things perfectly. One clean PR merged on day 5 is worth more than 10 PRs that need 3 rounds of review each.
Week 1 — Orientation and understanding
Day 1–7 Learn the codebase, team, and processes ▼
✓
Set up your development environment completely on day 1. Don't spend 3 days on this. Ask a teammate to pair with you for 30 min. Write down every step — you might onboard someone later.
✓
Read the codebase README and architecture docs before writing a single line of code. Understand: what does this service do? What does it talk to? What does it store?
✓
Trace one user request end-to-end through the codebase. Frontend → API → service layer → DB → response. This is the fastest way to understand a new codebase. Add console.log/breakpoints as you go.
✓
Understand the git workflow: how do PRs work? What's the branch naming convention? Who reviews? How long does review take? What does "done" mean here?
✓
Learn the deployment process: how does code get to production? CI/CD pipeline? Staging environment? Manual or automatic deploys?
✓
Meet your team: 1:1 coffee chats with each teammate. Ask: "What are you working on? What's the biggest challenge right now? Is there anything I can help with?" — people remember who showed genuine interest.
✓
Find a bug or doc improvement and raise a tiny PR. Even one typo fix. This shows you read the code, you understand the git workflow, and you're not waiting to be told what to do.
Week 2–3 — Deliver your first real contribution
Day 8–21 First feature or bug fix — done properly ▼
✓
Pick a clearly scoped task, not the largest open issue. First delivery should be something you can complete in 3–5 days. Size up before committing — "I think this is 2–3 days of work, does that align with expectations?"
✓
Ask clarifying questions before coding, not during review. "What should happen if the user passes an invalid ID? Should it return 404 or an empty list?" — saves 2 review cycles.
✓
Write tests for your code even if the team doesn't always. It shows you understand testing and dramatically reduces review feedback. Ask: "What testing patterns does this team use?"
✓
PR description matters: write what changed, why, how to test it, any edge cases considered. Reviewers who understand your thinking give faster, better feedback.
✓
Respond to review comments same day. Slow PR cycles frustrate senior engineers. If a comment is unclear: ask, don't assume. If you disagree: explain your reasoning and ask for their input — don't silently change it back.
✓
After your PR merges: verify it in staging. Watch logs after production deploy. If you broke something, own it immediately — say in Slack: "I think my PR caused this — I'm looking at it now." This builds trust faster than never breaking anything.
Week 4 — Solidify your presence
Day 22–30 Build habits and relationships that last ▼
✓
Schedule a 1:1 with your manager in week 3. Ask: "What are the top 2–3 things I should focus on in my first 3 months? What does success look like for someone in my role?" This sets explicit expectations — removes ambiguity about what "good" means.
✓
Find a technical mentor on the team — a senior engineer who's willing to answer questions. Ask them 1 technical question per week. Don't bombard them — come prepared with what you tried first.
✓
Start a work journal (private). Write daily: what did I do, what did I learn, what blocked me, what would I do differently? After 90 days this becomes your performance review evidence. Concrete wins, not vague feelings.
✓
Don't stop DSA practice. 1 LeetCode problem per day, 3 days per week minimum. The engineers who stop practicing after getting hired fall behind fast. Your next role's interview is happening in 2–3 years — don't lose the skill.
✓
Read code you didn't write. Spend 30 min per day reading existing codebase — not for a task, just to understand how senior engineers structure solutions. This is the fastest way to level up your code quality.
✓ The question to ask every 2 weeks
Ask your manager or a senior teammate: "Is there anything I'm doing that I should do differently? Anything I'm not doing that I should be?" Most people wait for a 6-month review to get this feedback. Getting it on week 2 and week 4 means you fix small issues before they become patterns. Engineers who ask for feedback early advance faster than those who wait.

Days 31–90 — From Surviving to Thriving

Month 2–3 is when you shift from "figuring things out" to "making things happen." This is when you start proposing improvements, not just implementing assigned tasks. Your reputation solidifies here.

Month 2 — Go deeper on your stack
Day 31–60 Own a feature end-to-end for the first time ▼
  • Own a full feature: frontend, backend, DB, tests, deployment. Not just a piece — the whole thing. This is what separates "implementer" from "engineer."
  • Write the tech spec first: a 1-page doc explaining what you're building, why, the approach, and trade-offs considered. Get feedback before writing code. This habit alone makes you a better engineer than 80% of freshers.
  • Observe production: watch your feature's metrics after release. API response times, error rates, user behavior. Connect your code to real-world impact.
  • Read post-mortems: when incidents happen (and they will), read the post-mortem docs carefully. This teaches you what production-scale systems actually break on — no textbook covers this.
  • Attend design discussions: even if you're not presenting. Listen to how senior engineers think through trade-offs. Take notes. Ask 1 good question per session.
Tech spec template — use for every non-trivial feature
  • Problem: What problem are we solving? For whom?
  • Proposed solution: What are we building? High-level approach.
  • Alternatives considered: What else could we do? Why this approach?
  • API changes: New endpoints, changed contracts, breaking vs non-breaking.
  • Database changes: Schema migrations, indexes, data migration plan.
  • Risk & rollback: What could go wrong? How do we revert if needed?
  • Testing plan: Unit tests, integration tests, manual testing steps.
Month 3 — Contribute beyond your assigned tickets
Day 61–90 Become someone the team relies on ▼
✓
Review other people's PRs. Even as a fresher, you can catch logic errors, suggest cleaner patterns, ask clarifying questions. This is how you build influence without authority.
✓
Fix something nobody asked you to fix. A recurring bug. A flaky test. Slow query. Unclear error message. Propose it first: "I noticed X causes Y — I'd like to fix it this sprint, takes about 2 hours." Small unprompted improvements make you memorable.
✓
Document something that isn't documented. The onboarding flow, a complex service, a tricky API. Good documentation is rare and valued. The person who writes good docs is trusted with more complex work.
✓
Propose at least 1 technical improvement. Add an index that speeds up a slow query. Suggest a caching layer for a repeatedly fetched resource. Even if it doesn't get accepted, proposing shows you think beyond your ticket scope.
✓
By day 90: you should know the system you work on well enough to explain it to a new joiner. If you can't explain it clearly, you don't understand it deeply enough yet.
📌 The 90-day self-evaluation
At day 90, sit down and honestly evaluate: Can I explain our system architecture to someone new? Do I know what breaks under load? Do I understand the business context for what I'm building? Am I someone my team trusts to take a task and see it through? If the answer to any of these is no — that's your next 90-day goal. Write it down and work toward it systematically.

Fresher → Mid-Level Engineer

In India's product company market, moving from SDE-1 to SDE-2 typically takes 1.5–3 years. The fastest path is deliberate: understand what the next level looks like, and work toward those specific criteria. Don't wait to be promoted — work at the level above and the promotion follows.

SDE-1 / Junior
Where you start
₹8–18 LPA in Bangalore
Technical expectations
  • Implements well-defined tasks with guidance
  • Writes code that works and passes review
  • Understands the immediate system you work in
  • Moderate DSA — LeetCode medium in interviews
  • Asks good clarifying questions before coding
Behavioral expectations
  • Completes tasks on time with minimal hand-holding
  • Communicates blockers early
  • Reviews code, gives useful feedback
  • Shows initiative on small improvements
SDE-2 / Mid-Level
Where you want to be in 2 years
₹20–40 LPA in Bangalore · competitive to join directly from companies
Technical expectations
  • Designs and implements features end-to-end without guidance
  • Writes tech specs. Identifies risks before they become incidents.
  • Understands multiple services and their interactions (distributed systems thinking)
  • Makes good trade-off decisions — knows when to use SQL vs NoSQL, when to cache, when to queue
  • Mentors juniors effectively. Reviews code with constructive, specific feedback.
  • Strong DSA — can solve hard LeetCode in interviews for the next role
How to get there from SDE-1
  • Own systems, not tickets. Proactively improve things nobody asked you to.
  • Develop technical breadth: understand infra, monitoring, databases, not just your service
  • Communicate upward: make your work visible. Write weekly updates. Present in team meetings.
  • Get a competing offer: often the fastest way to get a promotion. Market rate > internal rate at most companies.
  • Build expertise in 1–2 areas where you become the go-to person on the team
The 24-month learning plan — what to add on the job
Months 1–6 · Execution excellence
  • Deep expertise in your team's main tech stack. You should be the fastest person to debug issues in the services you own.
  • Testing discipline: write tests habitually. Learn your team's testing patterns and become someone who raises the quality bar.
  • Production awareness: learn monitoring tools (Datadog, Grafana, CloudWatch). Know your service's SLAs. Know what alerts fire and why.
  • Git mastery: clean commits, good PRs, rebasing, cherry-picking. Your git history should tell a clear story.
Months 7–12 · Scope expansion
  • Read system design books: "Designing Data-Intensive Applications" (DDIA) by Kleppmann. The best engineering book for SDE-1 → SDE-2. 1 chapter per week.
  • Understand adjacent services: how does your service fit into the larger system? What happens upstream and downstream?
  • Cloud/infra basics: Kubernetes basics, what happens when your pod gets restarted, how load balancers work at your company's scale.
  • Contribute to cross-team work: join a working group, contribute to platform improvements, write internal blog posts about your learnings.
Months 13–18 · Leadership signals
  • Lead a feature from start to finish: you write the spec, you coordinate with stakeholders, you run the sprint, you monitor after launch.
  • Onboard a new engineer: become the person who mentors the next fresher. Teaching forces you to articulate what you know implicitly.
  • Interview candidates: companies invite high performers to interview candidates. This is a signal of trust and accelerates your promotion case.
  • Prepare for interviews at other companies: keep your DSA sharp, update your projects, get 1–2 competing offers as salary calibration even if you don't want to leave.
Months 19–24 · Promotion or move
  • Build your promotion case proactively: document your contributions, impact, and scope expansion. Share with your manager 3 months before you want the promotion.
  • Have the compensation conversation: "I've been working at SDE-2 scope for 6 months. I'd like to discuss the path to SDE-2 title and compensation." Be direct.
  • If promotion isn't coming: use external offers. Most engineers get their biggest raises by switching companies. 40–60% jumps are common at the SDE-1 → SDE-2 level.
  • Your DSA should be strong enough to pass Tier-1 company interviews (CRED, Razorpay, Swiggy) as an experienced hire by this point.

Long-term Habits — What Separates Good from Great

These aren't study habits — they're career habits. The engineers who consistently grow are not smarter than average. They're more deliberate. They track their learning, build in public, and never stop improving their craft.

Technical habits — maintain these always
🧩
2 DSA problems per week — forever
Not 2 per day like during prep — but 2 per week is non-negotiable. Set a recurring calendar block: Saturday morning, 45 minutes, 2 medium problems. Your next job's interview is 2–3 years away. Don't let the skill atrophy. Engineers who stay sharp on DSA are comfortable with the idea of switching — which gives them leverage in compensation conversations too.
📖
Read 1 engineering blog post per day
Subscribe to: Swiggy Engineering, PhonePe Engineering, Grab Tech, Netflix TechBlog, Uber Engineering, Martin Fowler's blog. Read for 15 minutes each morning before Slack. Over 2 years this compounds into a huge advantage — you'll understand distributed systems, database internals, and scale from people who've actually built them at scale.
🔨
Build 1 small side project per quarter
Not a full application — a proof-of-concept, a utility tool, a CLI, a library. Something new that forces you to learn a technology you don't use at work. After 2 years: 8 small projects. Your creativity, breadth, and ability to ship independently compound. Many engineers use side projects to explore the technology of a company they want to join before interviewing there.
✍️
Write about what you learn — even briefly
LinkedIn post, internal wiki, personal blog, doesn't matter. The act of writing forces clarity. "I know it" and "I can explain it clearly in writing" are different things. 1 post per week about something you learned or built. After 2 years: 100 posts. Your professional brand becomes real. Recruiters from Tier-1 companies will find you.
📊
Track your market value every 6 months
Check levels.fyi, Glassdoor, LinkedIn Salary. Talk to friends at other companies. Do 1 exploratory interview at a company you're not sure about — just to understand the market. Know what you're worth. Engineers who never check their market value are systematically underpaid. Information is leverage.
Mental habits — what nobody talks about
🐢
Embrace being slow before being fast
New engineers try to look fast by typing quickly and asking few questions. Senior engineers look fast because they ask the right questions upfront and rarely do rework. The engineer who spends 20 minutes understanding the problem before writing a line of code delivers faster than the one who spends 2 hours writing the wrong thing. Slow down in the first 20 minutes. Speed up in the last 20.
🔍
Debug systematically, not randomly
Most junior engineers debug by changing random things until it works. This is the slowest possible approach. The scientific method for debugging: form a hypothesis → test it specifically → eliminate or confirm → next hypothesis. "I think the issue is in the middleware layer because the request reaches the controller but the response is wrong." Test that. Don't change 5 things at once.
🤝
Build relationships deliberately
The engineer who gets the interesting project, the promotion, the referral to the next company — it's almost always the one with good relationships. This doesn't mean being political. It means being genuinely curious about your colleagues' work, helping without being asked, and being someone people enjoy working with. Your network is your most durable career asset.
✓ The career compounding principle
The difference between a good engineer and a great one after 5 years is not talent — it's consistent, deliberate practice compounded. 2 DSA problems per week = 500 problems over 5 years. 1 blog post per week = 260 posts. 1 book per quarter = 20 technical books. These numbers seem small weekly. They're enormous compounded. Start now, even if slowly. Never completely stop.

✓ How to get the most out of AI mentoring
The quality of your AI session depends on the quality of your input. Vague questions get vague answers. "Explain React" gets a generic tutorial. "Explain why useMemo didn't fix my re-render problem in this specific component" gets a targeted, useful answer. Always: (1) give context about your current phase and target, (2) describe what you already tried, (3) ask for connection to your specific projects. The more specific your question, the better the answer.
